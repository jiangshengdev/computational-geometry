[Part 1: The Sweep and Three Types of Events](./why-neighbors.mdx) followed a complete sweep of four segments. This part is still Chapter 2. We now turn the rules illustrated by that example into reasoning that applies to every allowed input.

## Recall the Assumptions and Rules Needed for the Proof {/* #先回顾证明需要的条件与规则 */}

This chapter considers finitely many nonvertical segments of positive length: no overlaps, no endpoint contacts, and no point shared by three or more segments. All endpoints and intersections have distinct horizontal coordinates. Horizontal segments are allowed. These teaching assumptions help us see the basic mechanism; the complete algorithm must also handle more complicated inputs.

The sweep line is a vertical auxiliary line moving from left to right. An “active segment” is an input currently meeting it. Sorting the heights of those meetings from bottom to top gives the active order; two consecutive segments are neighbors. Endpoint and intersection positions are sweep events. Before and after an event, take the orders immediately to its left and right, respectively.

Initially, only endpoints are in the pending list. Each time, take the unprocessed position with the smallest horizontal coordinate. The three rules are: at a start, check the new segment's neighbors on both sides; at an end, check the two segments formerly separated by the removed segment; at a crossing, exchange the two segments and check the new outer adjacencies.

A check registers an intersection only after confirming that it lies within both segments and strictly to the right of the current sweep position. Keep one copy of each real future intersection until it is taken from the list; report a result only upon reaching the intersection. To connect these rules to individual events, return to the [event record table in Part 1](./why-neighbors.mdx#把每个事件放回同一张记录表).

## The Steps of This Proof {/* #这篇证明要走哪几步 */}

We need to connect four facts: order stays fixed when there is no event; two segments must be neighbors before crossing; new neighbors are checked when they form; therefore, there cannot be a first missed intersection. We first explain the algebra needed for the first fact, then use it in the geometric reasoning that follows.

## Start with Heights We Already Know How to Calculate {/* #从已经会算的高度谈起 */}

In Part 1, $A$ has height $1$ and $C$ has height $8-x$. For example, at $x=6$, the height of $C$ is $8-6=2$, above $A$. At $x=8$, its height is $8-8=0$, below $A$ instead.

We want notation that turns “which is higher?” into a number we can calculate. Then we can answer: **when their vertical order reverses, why must there be a position of equal height in between?**

Write $\teachBlue{y_A(x)}$ for the height of $\teachBlue{A}$ at horizontal coordinate $x$, and $\teachViolet{y_C(x)}$ for the height of $\teachViolet{C}$ at that same coordinate. The subscript tells us which segment; the number in parentheses tells us where to calculate. For example, $y_C(6)$ is the height obtained by substituting $x=6$ into $8-x$.

Always subtract the height of $A$ from that of $C$, and call the result $d_{CA}(x)$. The subscript $CA$ reminds us that the subtraction order remains $C$ minus $A$:

$$
d_{CA}(x)=\teachViolet{y_C(x)}-\teachBlue{y_A(x)}.
$$

Substitute the two heights to obtain $d_{CA}(x)=(8-x)-1$. Remove the parentheses to get $8-x-1$, then combine the constants $8-1$ to obtain $d_{CA}(x)=7-x$.

| Same horizontal coordinate | Height of $\teachViolet{C}$ | Height of $\teachBlue{A}$ | Always $C$ minus $A$ | Vertical relationship |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x=6$ | $8-6=2$ | $1$ | $d_{CA}(6)=2-1=1>0$ | $C$ is above $A$ |
| $x=7$ | $8-7=1$ | $1$ | $d_{CA}(7)=1-1=0$ | Equal heights; they intersect at $P$ |
| $x=8$ | $8-8=0$ | $1$ | $d_{CA}(8)=0-1=-1<0$ | $C$ is below $A$ |

A height difference can be positive, zero, or negative. It is not the nonnegative distance obtained by always subtracting “lower” from “higher.” If we switched whichever segment is higher to the front of the subtraction, we would lose the indication that their vertical order has reversed. The concrete calculation shows what the notation is for; next we must prove the result for any two segments.

## Write the Height Difference for Any Two Segments {/* #把任意两条线段的高度差写清楚 */}

Choose two nonvertical segments $U,V$. Write $y_U(x),y_V(x)$ for their respective heights at horizontal coordinate $x$. Whenever we discuss a range of horizontal coordinates, we will require both segments to exist throughout it, so that points on extended lines are not mistaken for points on the segments.

As explained in [Writing a Line Equation from Two Endpoints](./line-equation.mdx), the height on a nonvertical line can be written as “a fixed number times the horizontal coordinate, plus another fixed number.” Write the two containing lines separately:

$$
\teachBlue{y_U(x)}=\teachBlue{m_U}x+\teachBlue{b_U},
$$

$$
\teachOrange{y_V(x)}=\teachOrange{m_V}x+\teachOrange{b_V}.
$$

$m_U,b_U$ are the first line's two fixed coefficients, and $m_V,b_V$ are the second line's. Subscripts distinguish which line each belongs to. The horizontal coordinate $x$ can change, but these four coefficients do not change as the sweep moves.

For this chosen pair, always use $V$ minus $U$, and denote the height difference by $d(x)$:

$$
d(x)=\teachOrange{y_V(x)}-\teachBlue{y_U(x)}.
$$

Thus $d(x)>0$ means $V$ is above $U$, $d(x)=0$ means equal heights, and $d(x)<0$ means $V$ is below $U$. Earlier, $d_{CA}(x)$ specifically meant $C$ minus $A$ in the example; here $d(x)$ specifically means the chosen $V$ minus $U$.

To understand how $d(x)$ changes with position, first substitute the two line expressions:

$$
d(x)=(m_Vx+b_V)-(m_Ux+b_U).
$$

Subtracting the entire parenthesized expression changes the signs of both terms:

$$
d(x)=m_Vx+b_V-m_Ux-b_U.
$$

Group the terms containing $x$ together and the fixed constants together:

$$
d(x)=(m_Vx-m_Ux)+(b_V-b_U).
$$

Both terms in the first group contain $x$, so factor it out using the distributive law:

$$
d(x)=(m_V-m_U)x+(b_V-b_U).
$$

Only now introduce abbreviations for the two coefficient differences. Let the Greek letters $\alpha$ (alpha) and $\beta$ (beta) denote

$$
\alpha=m_V-m_U,\qquad \beta=b_V-b_U.
$$

Then

$$
d(x)=\alpha x+\beta.
$$

$\beta$ is the difference between the two lines' constant terms; it is a different quantity from either $b_U$ or $b_V$. Each has its own notation. This form lets us compare the heights by studying a single height-difference expression, without writing both lines at every step.

### First Handle a Constant Height Difference {/* #先处理高度差不变的情况 */}

If $\alpha=0$, then $d(x)=0\times x+\beta=\beta$. Whatever the horizontal coordinate, the height difference is the same number.

When $\beta>0$, $V$ remains above $U$; when $\beta<0$, $V$ remains below $U$. Neither case can reverse the order.

When $\beta=0$, the equation $\alpha=m_V-m_U=0$ gives $m_V=m_U$, and $\beta=b_V-b_U=0$ gives $b_V=b_U$. The two containing lines coincide completely. If the segments also both cover a horizontal-coordinate interval of positive length, their heights match at every position in that interval, producing a nonzero-length overlap. This chapter excludes overlaps, so this situation cannot occur on the common interval we are about to discuss.

What remains to study is a height difference that changes sign. Then $\alpha$ cannot be zero. Our goal is to find a position in between where the difference equals zero.

## Use a Ratio to Describe an Intermediate Position {/* #用一个比例表示中间的位置 */}

Choose two horizontal coordinates: $u$ on the left and $v$ on the right, so $u<v$. Suppose $U,V$ both exist throughout the interval from $u$ to $v$, with both ends also within each segment's bounds.

$d(u)$ means substituting position $u$ into the height-difference expression: $d(u)=\alpha u+\beta$. Similarly, $d(v)=\alpha v+\beta$. The $u,v$ in parentheses are not multipliers; they tell us “at which horizontal coordinate to calculate.”

First assume $d(u)>0$ and $d(v)<0$: on the left, $V$ is above $U$; on the right, it is below $U$. We know the differences at the two ends and want to find where the difference is zero between them. For this, we first need a way to describe every intermediate position.

### First Walk Along a Number Line {/* #先在数轴上走一小段 */}

Consider a separate numerical example: moving from horizontal coordinate $2$ to $10$, the full distance is $10-2=8$. After covering one quarter of it, the distance traveled is $\frac14\times8=2$, and the current position is

$$
2+2=4.
$$

Here $4$ is between $2$ and $10$. To obtain the current position, calculating the distance traveled is not enough; we must add the starting position.

The shorter marked line shows the distance traveled, and the longer one shows the full distance. Both are measured from the same starting point. They are drawn above and below the number line to distinguish their lengths, not to represent two different paths.

### Now Use General Notation {/* #再换成一般记号 */}

From $u$ to $v$, the full distance is $v-u$, which is positive because $v>u$. Let $t$ denote “the fraction of the full distance already traveled”: for example, $t=\frac14$ means one quarter. The traveled distance is $t(v-u)$. Add the starting position to obtain the current position:

$$
u+t(v-u).
$$

<IntersectionFigures topic="why-neighbors-proof" prefix="sweep-proof" name="ratio-general"
  panels={['General number line: start u, end v, full distance v-u; distance traveled t(v-u), current position u+t(v-u). The diagram uses 0<t<1.']}
/>

When $t=0$, the distance traveled is zero and we remain at $u$. When $t=1$, the position is $u+(v-u)=v$. If $0<t<1$, multiplying the inequality by positive $v-u$ gives

$$
0<t(v-u)<v-u.
$$

Add $u$ to all three parts:

$$
u<u+t(v-u)<u+(v-u)=v.
$$

Thus, **finding a ratio $t$ strictly between $0$ and $1$ gives a position strictly between $u,v$.** Next we want the height difference at this position to be exactly zero.

## Calculate Where the Height Difference Is Zero {/* #算出哪个位置的高度差为零 */}

### Why Rewrite It Using the Height Differences at the Ends? {/* #为什么要把式子改写成两端的高度差 */}

We know $d(u)>0$ and $d(v)<0$, but not the difference in between. The purpose of rewriting is therefore to express the intermediate difference in terms of $d(u),d(v)$, so that we can use these known signs.

In $d(x)=\alpha x+\beta$, replace $x$ with the position just described:

$$
d\bigl(u+t(v-u)\bigr)=\alpha\bigl(u+t(v-u)\bigr)+\beta.
$$

Expand the outer parentheses using the distributive law:

$$
d\bigl(u+t(v-u)\bigr)=\alpha u+\alpha t(v-u)+\beta.
$$

Group $\alpha u$ with $\beta$. In the remaining product, change the factor order to put $t$ first:

$$
d\bigl(u+t(v-u)\bigr)=(\alpha u+\beta)+t\alpha(v-u).
$$

Expand $\alpha(v-u)=\alpha v-\alpha u$:

$$
d\bigl(u+t(v-u)\bigr)=(\alpha u+\beta)+t(\alpha v-\alpha u).
$$

The first parenthesized expression is already $d(u)$. To recognize the second, separately calculate the difference between the height differences at the ends:

$$
d(v)-d(u)=(\alpha v+\beta)-(\alpha u+\beta).
$$

Remove parentheses, then group the constant terms:

$$
d(v)-d(u)=\alpha v+\beta-\alpha u-\beta
=(\alpha v-\alpha u)+(\beta-\beta).
$$

Since $\beta-\beta=0$, we have $d(v)-d(u)=\alpha v-\alpha u$. Substitute these two identified expressions back:

$$
d\bigl(u+t(v-u)\bigr)=\teachBlue{d(u)}+t\bigl(\teachOrange{d(v)}-\teachBlue{d(u)}\bigr).
$$

This says that the height difference at an intermediate position equals the left-end height difference plus $t$ times the change between the two ends. It is not an extra assumption: it is the equality we have just expanded step by step, and it holds for every ratio $t$.

### Set the Difference to Zero, Then Check the Ratio {/* #令这个差等于零再检查比例是否合适 */}

Equal heights mean a zero height difference. Set the right-hand side above to zero:

$$
0=d(u)+t\bigl(d(v)-d(u)\bigr).
$$

Subtract $d(u)$ from both sides:

$$
-d(u)=t\bigl(d(v)-d(u)\bigr).
$$

Multiply both sides by $-1$, changing the signs of both terms inside the right-hand parentheses:

$$
d(u)=t\bigl(-d(v)+d(u)\bigr).
$$

Exchange the order of those two terms to write

$$
d(u)=t\bigl(d(u)-d(v)\bigr).
$$

Since $d(v)<0$, we have $-d(v)>0$. Also $d(u)>0$, so

$$
d(u)-d(v)=d(u)+\bigl(-d(v)\bigr)>d(u)>0.
$$

Thus $d(u)-d(v)$ is nonzero, and we may divide both sides of the equation by it:

$$
\frac{d(u)}{d(u)-d(v)}=t,
$$

or

$$
t=\frac{\teachBlue{d(u)}}{\teachBlue{d(u)}-\teachOrange{d(v)}}.
$$

We must also check that this solution does not place us outside the two ends. Both numerator and denominator are positive, so $t>0$. The numerator $d(u)$ is smaller than the denominator $d(u)-d(v)$. Divide both sides of that inequality by this positive denominator:

$$
\frac{d(u)}{d(u)-d(v)}<\frac{d(u)-d(v)}{d(u)-d(v)}=1.
$$

Therefore $0<t<1$. The earlier number-line reasoning tells us that $u+t(v-u)$ lies strictly between $u,v$. Substituting this $t$ back into the zero-difference equation indeed makes the difference zero. Since both segments exist throughout the interval, this equal-height position is an intersection of the two **segments**.

### What If the Difference Changes from Negative to Positive? {/* #如果是从负变正呢 */}

Keep $d(x)=y_V(x)-y_U(x)$; do not switch the subtraction order. Now assume $d(u)<0$ and $d(v)>0$. The earlier substitutions, expansions, and rearranged equations still hold. Only the justification for division and the inequalities changes.

Since $d(u)<0<d(v)$, we have $d(u)-d(v)<0$, which is still nonzero. The equation gives the same ratio formula. To see its size clearly, multiply numerator and denominator by the nonzero number $-1$:

$$
t=\frac{d(u)}{d(u)-d(v)}
=\frac{-d(u)}{-(d(u)-d(v))}
=\frac{-d(u)}{d(v)-d(u)}.
$$

The last step distributes the negative sign in the denominator, giving $-d(u)+d(v)=d(v)-d(u)$. Now the numerator $-d(u)>0$, and the denominator $d(v)-d(u)=d(v)+(-d(u))$ is greater than the numerator. Hence

$$
0<-d(u)<d(v)-d(u).
$$

Divide all three parts by positive $d(v)-d(u)$ to obtain $0<t<1$ again. Thus a change from negative to positive also produces a segment intersection strictly between the ends. If the height difference at an end is already zero, that end is already an intersection; the two sign-change cases here address endpoints whose differences are both nonzero.

## Without an Event, the Order Cannot Change {/* #没有事件就不会改变顺序 */}

We have now proved: **if both segments exist throughout an interval and their vertical order is reversed at its ends, there must be an intersection inside the interval.** We handled both directions of sign change, as well as the case of a constant height difference.

Now consider a horizontal-coordinate range with no endpoint and no intersection. With no endpoint, no segment starts or ends, so the active membership stays fixed. If some pair changed its vertical order, we could choose a position before and one after the change where the height difference, taken in the same subtraction order, has opposite signs. By the result just proved, there must be an intersection in between, contradicting the absence of intersections in this range.

Thus every pair's vertical relationship stays fixed, and so does the entire active order. **We need to update the order only at sweep events, not re-sort after every tiny movement.** Next we use this fact to investigate whether a third segment can remain between two segments just before they cross.

## Why Must Two Segments Become Neighbors Before Crossing? {/* #两条线段交叉前为什么一定成为邻居 */}

The preceding result applies to any pair. Now suppose the chosen $U,V$ intersect, and let $p$ be the intersection's horizontal coordinate. Here $p$ is one coordinate value, not the intersection's pair of coordinates. The heights there are $y_U(p),y_V(p)$. Intersecting means $y_U(p)=y_V(p)$.

We will prove that, in a short interval before reaching $p$, no other active segment lies between $U,V$. They do not have to be neighbors from the beginning.

### Why Can We Choose an Interval Before the Intersection with No Other Event? {/* #为什么能选出交点前没有其他事件的一段 */}

There are finitely many inputs, each with two endpoints. Since there are no overlaps, each segment pair has at most one intersection. Thus the total number of endpoints and intersections is finite. Here we mean all actual events in the input; we can discuss their geometry without requiring the algorithm to have discovered them first.

Let $e$ be the horizontal coordinate of the last event to the left of $p$. Such an event exists because both left endpoints of $U,V$ lie left of $p$, and a finite set of coordinates has a greatest member. Event coordinates are all distinct, so $e<p$.

Choose $r=(e+p)/2$ between them. Why is it between them? Subtract separately:

$$
r-e=\frac{e+p-2e}{2}=\frac{p-e}{2}>0,
$$

$$
p-r=\frac{2p-e-p}{2}=\frac{p-e}{2}>0.
$$

Both inequalities follow from $p-e>0$ and divisor $2>0$. Thus $e<r<p$, and there is no other endpoint or intersection from $r$ up to, but not including, $p$.

The intersection of $U,V$ is in both interiors because endpoint contacts are excluded. Their left endpoints are no later than $e$, and their right endpoints lie to the right of $p$. Hence $U,V$ both exist throughout the interval from $r$ to $p$.

By “no event, no order change,” active membership and vertical order remain fixed from $r$ up to the left side of $p$. We can now ask whether some other segment can lie between $U,V$ in this stable order.

### Suppose for Now That a Segment W Lies Between Them {/* #暂时假设夹着一条-w会发生什么 */}

To rule out this possibility, assume temporarily that an active segment $W$ lies between them at $r$. This is a “proof by contradiction”: we temporarily accept the situation we want to exclude. If it necessarily violates a known condition, the assumption cannot be true.

Write $y_W(x)$ for the height of $W$ at horizontal coordinate $x$. First consider $U$ below and $V$ above. At $r$ we have

$$
y_U(r)<y_W(r)<y_V(r).
$$

<IntersectionFigures topic="why-neighbors-proof" prefix="sweep-proof" name="between-assumption"
  panels={['Temporary assumption for contradiction: at r, to the left of intersection coordinate p, W lies between U and V. There is no other event from r up to p.']}
/>

**This figure shows the assumption we aim to rule out.** It initially draws only a short part of $W$ near $r$, without deciding its height at $p$. Dashed lines $r,p$ mark the range of the argument. This is not a set of inputs already confirmed to satisfy the chapter's assumptions.

First confirm that $W$ cannot disappear. It is already active at $r$. If it ended between $r$ and $p$, that would be an endpoint event, contradicting the absence of events in this range. If it ended exactly at $p$, its endpoint would share the horizontal coordinate of the intersection of $U,V$, also violating the assumptions. Thus $W$ exists all the way to $p$.

Next confirm that it cannot move outside the pair before reaching $p$. There is no event from $r$ up to $p$, so the active order stays fixed: $W$ remains above $U$ and below $V$. Only one question remains: **at the boundary position $p$ itself, could it suddenly lie outside the two segments?**

### Where Can the Height of W Be at the Intersection? {/* #到交点时w-的高度被限制在哪里 */}

If $y_W(p)<y_U(p)$, then the height difference $W$ minus $U$ is positive at $r$ and negative at $p$. Both segments exist throughout the interval from $r$ to $p$. By the sign-change result proved earlier, $W,U$ must intersect strictly between $r,p$. This contradicts the absence of other events. Therefore $y_W(p)<y_U(p)$ is impossible, meaning

$$
y_U(p)\le y_W(p).
$$

If $y_W(p)>y_V(p)$, the height difference $W$ minus $V$ is negative at $r$ and positive at $p$. Applying the same result for the opposite sign-change direction gives an intersection of $W,V$ strictly between $r,p$, again a contradiction. Thus $y_W(p)>y_V(p)$ is impossible, meaning

$$
y_W(p)\le y_V(p).
$$

Combine these restrictions:

$$
y_U(p)\le y_W(p)\le y_V(p).
$$

But $U,V$ are intersecting, so $y_U(p)=y_V(p)$. A number that can be neither smaller nor greater than this common height must equal it. Therefore

$$
y_U(p)=y_W(p)=y_V(p).
$$

<IntersectionFigures topic="why-neighbors-proof" prefix="sweep-proof" name="between-contradiction"
  panels={['Contradiction forced by the between-segments assumption: at p, W can be neither below U nor above V, so it must have the same height as both, giving a point shared by three segments.']}
/>

**This figure is the consequence forced by the preceding assumption, not an input allowed in this chapter.** Three segments with the same horizontal coordinate and the same height pass through the same point, violating the condition that no point belongs to three or more segments. The figure illustrates the contradiction; its justification remains the two height-difference arguments above.

If $V$ is below and $U$ above before the crossing, then $W$ minus $V$ is positive at $r$, and $W$ minus $U$ is negative there. In the same way, rule out the former becoming negative and the latter becoming positive at $p$. This gives $y_V(p)\le y_W(p)\le y_U(p)$. The two ends are still equal, again forcing a point shared by three segments. Neither vertical order permits a segment $W$ in between.

Thus we must reject the initial assumption that “a $W$ still lies between them.” **Two segments must become neighbors before crossing, although they need not be neighbors from the start.** This is a geometric statement about adjacency, not yet a proof that the sweep actually checked the pair. The next section supplies that connection.

## Why Are New Neighbors Always Checked When They Form? {/* #新邻居形成时为什么一定接受检查 */}

At the beginning, the active order is empty and there are no neighbors. Without an event, neither membership nor order changes, so a new neighboring pair cannot appear on its own. Adjacencies can therefore form only at the three event types.

A start event inserts one segment into the existing order. The relative heights of old segments stay fixed, as do adjacencies away from the insertion position. The only new adjacencies are between the new segment and one segment below and one above. The start rule checks exactly these two sides, skipping a side that does not exist.

An end event removes one segment. The order of all other members stays fixed. If it had a neighbor on each side, the barrier between those two disappears and they become neighbors. The end rule checks exactly this pair. If there was a neighbor on only one side, removing the segment cannot join two old members.

The two segments involved in a crossing are already neighbors before it, as the previous section proved. If a short consecutive portion of the order before crossing is $L,U,V,H$, where $L$ is the lower outer neighbor and $H$ the upper outer neighbor, afterward it is $L,V,U,H$. The only new adjacencies are $L,V$ and $U,H$, exactly the two pairs checked by the crossing rule; skip nonexistent outer neighbors. $U,V$ merely exchange vertical positions. This pair has not become a different input pair, and there is no need to seek a second intersection.

We have also used the fact that crossing reverses their order. Check it from $d(x)=\alpha x+\beta$: two distinct lines intersect at $p$, so $\alpha p+\beta=0$. Subtract $\alpha p$ from both sides to obtain $\beta=-\alpha p$. Substitution gives $d(x)=\alpha x-\alpha p$; factoring out $\alpha$ gives $d(x)=\alpha(x-p)$. Here $\alpha$ cannot be zero, since then $\beta$ would also be zero and the lines would coincide, contrary to the no-overlap condition in this situation. The factor $x-p$ is negative left of $p$ and positive right of it. Multiplying by fixed nonzero $\alpha$ still gives opposite signs on the two sides, so the segments really do exchange order. Other segments neither start nor end here and do not pass through this intersection, so their order stays fixed.

We obtain a conditional statement about the process: **provided all earlier events have been processed correctly under the rules, every new neighboring pair is checked when it forms.** Why include “earlier”? We are still proving that no intersection is missed, so we cannot assume in advance that “all crossings have been processed.” Next we use only events to the left of a chosen position, avoiding circular reasoning.

## Why Can There Be No First Missed Intersection? {/* #为什么不可能有第一个漏掉的交点 */}

Now connect “neighbors before crossing” with “new neighbors are checked” to prove that the sweep list misses no intersection.

### Why Choose the Leftmost Missed Intersection? {/* #为什么要挑最靠左的漏点 */}

Start a proof by contradiction: temporarily suppose that after the sweep ends, at least one real intersection has been missed. “Missed” means that the intersection of its input pair was not reported upon reaching that position.

There are finitely many intersections, so finitely many missed ones. Choose the one with smallest horizontal coordinate. Denote that coordinate by $p_*$, and the two inputs crossing there by $U_*,V_*$. The subscript $*$ simply marks “the selected earliest missed intersection and its two segments”; it is not multiplication. These symbols are distinct from $p,U,V$, used earlier for an arbitrary intersection.

Choosing the smallest has this consequence: **every real intersection strictly left of $p_*$ was not missed.** We assume nothing about the right side, nor do we assume that $p_*$ has already been discovered.

All endpoints enter the list at the start, so endpoints on the left cannot be omitted either. Starting with the empty order on the far left, consider events to the left of $p_*$ in position order: insert at a start, remove at an end, and exchange at a crossing. Each step is processed under the rules. Between consecutive events there is no other event, so order stays fixed. Therefore, up to the left side of $p_*$, the maintained active order matches the actual geometric order. Every intersection used in this reasoning is left of $p_*$, precisely the range guaranteed by choosing the earliest missed one.

### Find When the Pair Became Adjacent {/* #找到它们形成邻接的时刻 */}

The earlier geometric result applies to every intersection, including this one: $U_*,V_*$ must be neighbors in a short interval before $p_*$.

The initial order was empty, so this adjacency cannot have existed before the sweep began. Adjacency changes only at events. Tracing the event sequence, we can therefore find the event that created the adjacency that persists until just before $p_*$. Call that event's horizontal coordinate $s$. If they were neighbors earlier and were later separated by another segment, use the last event where they became neighbors again.

This position is strictly left of the intersection: $s<p_*$. It has already been processed correctly. Under the three rules, $U_*,V_*$ were checked when this adjacency formed. What does that check imply?

### Follow the Check All the Way to Reporting {/* #从检查一直追到报告 */}

Check the consequences and the pending-list rules one by one:

1. The point at $p_*$ really is an intersection of $U_*,V_*$. Thus the common point obtained by solving their line equations is within both segments' bounds. It cannot be rejected by the bounds check as the intersection of the extended lines of $B,C$ was in Part 1.
2. The check occurs at $s$, and $s<p_*$. Hence this intersection lies strictly in the future and cannot be rejected as “already reached or passed.”
3. Therefore, if not yet registered, it must enter the pending list. If it was registered earlier, the list already has that record and no duplicate is needed. In either case, it is in the list.
4. This chapter's rules retain confirmed real future intersections even when their segments temporarily stop being neighbors. The record therefore cannot disappear before reaching $p_*$.
5. Each time, the list yields the leftmost unprocessed position. There are finitely many endpoints and real intersections, and the same event is not registered repeatedly, so the pending events left of $p_*$ will all be taken out. Since $p_*$ remains in the list, the sweep cannot stop or skip past it to a position farther right. It must reach $p_*$ and report the corresponding input pair.

The hollow circle denotes the intersection retained in the list before it is reached, and the filled circle means reaching and reporting it under the rules. “Reporting” here is the necessary consequence of the argument. It directly contradicts the assumption that this intersection was missed.

There can therefore be no first missed intersection. If there were any missed intersections, their finite set would have a first one. Since the first is impossible, **no intersection is missed**.

## Why Are There No False or Duplicate Reports? {/* #为什么也不会多报或重复 */}

Missing nothing is only part of correctness. We must also show that every reported result is real and no input pair is reported twice.

No false reports: only real intersections computed and confirmed to lie within both segments are registered. The corresponding pair's intersection is reported upon reaching it. Points belonging only to extended lines, not to the segments, cannot enter the results.

No duplicate reports: this chapter excludes overlaps, so two segments have at most one intersection. Each crossing is registered only once, and repeated checks do not create a second record. Upon arrival, it is taken out and reported. If the pair is checked again later, that point is no longer strictly in the future and cannot be added again. Thus each original unordered input pair is reported once.

A result's identity here is its input pair. $U_*,V_*$ and $V_*,U_*$ are the same unordered pair; other input pairs have their own identities. Although this chapter excludes points shared by multiple segments, that does not make “same geometric position” a general synonym for “same input pair.” Chapter 1 showed that more complicated inputs can yield results for several distinct pairs at one position.

## What Work Is Saved, and What Comes Next? {/* #省下的是什么下一章还要补什么 */}

The sweep line saves the work of “rechecking every segment pair whenever anything changes.” A start checks at most two new neighboring pairs, an end at most one, and an ordinary crossing at most two. Without an event, the active order needs no update.

This does not mean the total check count is fixed, nor that every intersection computation performed by pairwise checking is avoided. In our example, following adjacency changes checks $A,C$ and $A,D$ again. If there are many intersections, there are inherently many results to report. Maintaining the order and pending list also costs work. Chapter 10 will bring these costs together.

This part also relies on several decisions: which segment is higher, where an intersection lies, whether it remains inside both segments, and whether two records describe the same event. The example uses integer endpoints and integer intersections to keep attention on adjacency changes. The next chapter, “Keeping Geometric Decisions Exact,” considers more general coordinates and explains how to make these decisions reliably.
