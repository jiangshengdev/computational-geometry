This article starts with coordinates and the linear functions learned in middle school. Our question is: **given the two endpoints of a segment, how can we use an equation to describe its containing line?**

## First Understand What It Means for a Point to Satisfy an Equation {/* #先看懂点满足一个等式 */}

The point $(1,3)$ has horizontal coordinate $1$ and vertical coordinate $3$. Below, $X$ denotes the horizontal coordinate and $Y$ the vertical coordinate. These capital letters work just like the lowercase $x,y$ in textbooks.

Consider this equation:

$$
Y=2X+1.
$$

It says: **the vertical coordinate is $2$ times the horizontal coordinate, plus $1$.**

| Horizontal coordinate $X$ | Calculate the vertical coordinate $\teachBlue{Y}=\teachBlue{2X+1}$ | Resulting point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $2\times0+1=1$ | $(0,1)$ |
| $1$ | $2\times1+1=3$ | $(1,3)$ |
| $2$ | $2\times2+1=5$ | $(2,5)$ |
| $3$ | $2\times3+1=7$ | $(3,7)$ |

The four dots below plot the four points in the table. They all lie on the blue line. A cross additionally marks $(2,6)$ for comparison.

<ThemedImage
  alt="Four dots, (0,1), (1,3), (2,5), and (3,7), lie on Y=2X+1. The cross at (2,6) is 1 above (2,5) at the same horizontal coordinate and is not on the line."
  sources={{
    light: useBaseUrl('/img/segment-intersection/line-equation/light/line-equation-points-light.svg'),
    dark: useBaseUrl('/img/segment-intersection/line-equation/dark/line-equation-points-dark.svg'),
  }}
/>

Look at $X=2$ in the figure. The equation requires $Y=2\times2+1=5$, so $(2,5)$ lies on the line. The point $(2,6)$, which is $1$ higher, does not, since $6\ne2\times2+1$. **Sharing a horizontal coordinate does not mean both points satisfy the equation; the vertical coordinate must also meet its requirement.**

Only finitely many points are marked. From linear functions in middle school, we know that letting $X$ range over all real numbers, including negative numbers and decimals, gives an entire straight line as the graph of $Y=2X+1$.

This equality is therefore called the line's “equation.” To test whether a point lies on the line, substitute both coordinates and check whether the equality holds.

## How Can We Find the Equation from Two Endpoints? {/* #已知两个端点怎样求出这个等式 */}

Now reverse the question. Given only the endpoints $\teachBlue{A}=(1,3)$ and $\teachBlue{B}=(3,7)$, how do we find the equation of their line?

First consider how much each coordinate changes from $A$ to $B$. The dashed lines split the change into two parts: move right by $3-1=2$, then up by $7-3=4$. These are auxiliary reading guides, not the original segment.

<ThemedImage
  alt="Move horizontally right from A=(1,3) to (3,3), increasing the horizontal coordinate by 2, then vertically up to B=(3,7), increasing the vertical coordinate by 4. The slope of line AB is 4 divided by 2, which equals 2."
  sources={{
    light: useBaseUrl('/img/segment-intersection/line-equation/light/line-equation-slope-light.svg'),
    dark: useBaseUrl('/img/segment-intersection/line-equation/dark/line-equation-slope-dark.svg'),
  }}
/>

Two distinct points determine a line. Since this line is not vertical, start with the linear-function form

$$
Y=kX+d.
$$

Here $k,d$ are two unknown numbers. Textbooks often use $b$ for the last number. We use $d$ for now because $b$ will appear in another form later.

The line passes through $A=(1,3)$, so substitution must give a true equation:

$$
3=k+d.
$$

It also passes through $B=(3,7)$, so

$$
7=3k+d.
$$

Subtract the first equation from the second to eliminate $d$:

$$
7-3=(3k+d)-(k+d),
$$

which gives $4=2k$. The $4$ and $2$ are exactly the upward and rightward changes in the figure: a horizontal increase of $2$ gives a vertical increase of $2k$, and we know that increase is $4$. Divide both sides by $2$ to obtain $k=2$. This number is the “slope.” Here it means moving $1$ to the right along the line takes us $2$ upward.

Substitute back into $3=k+d$ to get $3=2+d$. Subtract $2$ from both sides to obtain $d=1$.

The line equation is therefore

$$
Y=2X+1.
$$

## Why Also Write aX+bY=c? {/* #为什么又写成-axbyc */}

Rewrite the same equation. First move $2X$ to the left:

$$
Y-2X=1.
$$

Then multiply both sides by $-1$:

$$
2X-Y=-1.
$$

Neither transformation changes the equation's solutions, so it still describes the same line.

Call the number before $X$ by the name $a$, the number before $Y$ by $b$, and the number on the right by $c$. Then we can write the general form

$$
aX+bY=c.
$$

In $2X-Y=-1$, we have $a=2$, $b=-1$, and $c=-1$. Remember that $-Y$ means $(-1)\times Y$, so $b$ is $-1$ here.

**$a,b,c$ are three fixed numbers; $X,Y$ are the coordinates of a point on the line and can vary.**

## Can We Calculate a, b, c Directly from the Endpoints? {/* #能不能直接从端点算出-abc */}

Yes, without first finding $k,d$ in the linear-function form. However, “calculate” does not mean that there is only one possible triple $a,b,c$. We need to find a triple making $aX+bY=c$ describe the line through the two endpoints.

Keep using $A=(1,3)$ and $B=(3,7)$. Start with the equalities these two points must satisfy, rather than rushing to memorize a formula.

### What Requirements Do the Two Points Place on a and b? {/* #两个点给-ab-提出了什么要求 */}

The line passes through $A=(1,3)$, so substituting $X=1$, $Y=3$ into $aX+bY=c$ must make the equality true:

$$
a\times1+b\times3=c.
$$

That is,

$$
a+3b=c.
$$

The line also passes through $B=(3,7)$. Substituting $X=3$, $Y=7$ gives

$$
a\times3+b\times7=c,
$$

or

$$
3a+7b=c.
$$

Both equations have the same number $c$ on the right. Subtract the first from the second to eliminate it:

$$
(3a+7b)-(a+3b)=c-c.
$$

Remove the parentheses. When subtracting an entire parenthesized expression, change the signs of both terms:

$$
3a+7b-a-3b=0.
$$

Group the $a$ terms and the $b$ terms:

$$
(3a-a)+(7b-3b)=0.
$$

Combine like terms:

$$
2a+4b=0.
$$

Look back at the dashed-line diagram. From $A$ to $B$, the horizontal coordinate increases by $3-1=2$ and the vertical coordinate by $7-3=4$. Thus the total change in $aX+bY$ is $2a+4b$. **Both substituted points must give the same $c$, so this change must equal $0$.**

### Find One Triple, Then See Why It Is Not Unique {/* #先求出一组再看为什么答案不唯一 */}

Starting from $2a+4b=0$, subtract $4b$ from both sides:

$$
2a=-4b.
$$

Divide both sides by the nonzero number $2$:

$$
a=-2b.
$$

This says that once we choose $b$, $a$ is $-2$ times $b$. It does not prescribe a particular value for $b$.

In this example, we cannot choose $b=0$, because that would also make $a=0$. Substituting an endpoint would then give $c=0$, and the equation would become $0=0$. Every point satisfies it, so it cannot describe the line we want.

First choose an easy value, $b=-1$. Substitute into $a=-2b$:

$$
a=(-2)\times(-1)=2.
$$

The equation is now

$$
2X-Y=c.
$$

Only $c$ remains unknown. The first point $(1,3)$ lies on the line, so substituting its coordinates must make the equation true:

$$
2\times1-3=c.
$$

First multiply to get $2-3=c$, then subtract to get $-1=c$, or $c=-1$. We have found an equation:

$$
2X-Y=-1.
$$

Check the second point $(3,7)$: the left side is $2\times3-7=6-7=-1$, equal to the right side too.

Multiplying both sides of this equation by $2$ gives

$$
2(2X-Y)=2\times(-1).
$$

Expand the left side using the distributive law and calculate the right side:

$$
4X-2Y=-2.
$$

Now $a=4$, $b=-2$, and $c=-2$. Multiplication by $2$ can be reversed by dividing both sides by $2$, so exactly the same points satisfy the two equations. **$(2,-1,-1)$ and $(4,-2,-2)$ are different coefficient triples describing the same line.**

### Choose a and b Directly to Avoid That Division {/* #怎样直接选择-ab省去刚才的除法 */}

We first chose $b=-1$ and later multiplied the whole equation by $2$ to explain why the coefficients are not unique. In an actual calculation, we can directly choose $a=4$, $b=-2$ from the latter triple.

Why choose these numbers? Look again at the condition imposed by the endpoints:

$$
2a+4b=0.
$$

We want the two terms on the left to cancel. Multiplication is commutative: $2\times4$ equals $4\times2$. Therefore, choosing $a=4$ in the first term and $b=-2$ in the second makes the left side

$$
2\times4+4\times(-2)=8+(-8)=0.
$$

This is a choice requiring no division: **take $a$ as the change in vertical coordinate, and $b$ as the negative of the change in horizontal coordinate.** Here the vertical change is $7-3=4$, so $a=4$. The horizontal change is $3-1=2$, whose negative is $-(3-1)=-2$, so $b=-2$. We can also write that negative as $1-3$, which explains the reversed subtraction order in the second coefficient.

Having chosen these two numbers, the equation is temporarily

$$
4X-2Y=c.
$$

**The right side $c$ must equal the value obtained by substituting any point on the line into the left side.** We already know the first point $(1,3)$ lies on the line, so use it to calculate. Substitute $X=1$, $Y=3$:

$$
c=4\times1+(-2)\times3.
$$

First calculate the two products: $4\times1=4$ and $(-2)\times3=-6$. Then add:

$$
c=4+(-6)=-2.
$$

We are not multiplying the coordinates of the two different points together. We multiply the first point's horizontal coordinate by $a$, its vertical coordinate by $b$, and add the results. Using the second point also works: $4\times3+(-2)\times7=12+(-14)=-2$, the same result.

### Write This Choice as a General Formula {/* #把这个取法写成通用公式 */}

First name the coordinates of the two points:

| Symbol | Meaning | Value in this example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $X_1$ | Horizontal coordinate of the first point | $1$ |
| $Y_1$ | Vertical coordinate of the first point | $3$ |
| $X_2$ | Horizontal coordinate of the second point | $3$ |
| $Y_2$ | Vertical coordinate of the second point | $7$ |

The subscripts $1,2$ are just labels distinguishing the two points; $X_1$ does not mean $X\times1$.

Write the first point as $(X_1,Y_1)$ and the second as $(X_2,Y_2)$. From the first to the second, the horizontal change is $X_2-X_1$ and the vertical change is $Y_2-Y_1$.

Following the choice above, first take $a$ as the vertical change:

$$
a=Y_2-Y_1,
$$

then take $b$ as the negative of the horizontal change. Removing parentheses preceded by a minus sign changes both signs:

$$
b=-(X_2-X_1)=X_1-X_2.
$$

Finally, because the first point is on the line, substitute $X=X_1$, $Y=Y_1$ into $aX+bY=c$ to obtain

$$
c=a\times X_1+b\times Y_1.
$$

Omitting multiplication signs, the last line is $c=aX_1+bY_1$. It still means “substitute the first point into the left side to calculate what the right side must be.”

The endpoints must be distinct so that $a,b$ are not both $0$. If $a=Y_2-Y_1=0$, their vertical coordinates are equal. If $b=X_1-X_2=0$, their horizontal coordinates are equal too. If both hold, the points coincide.

These three steps use only addition, subtraction, and multiplication. Integer endpoint coordinates therefore give integer $a,b,c$. Next we prove why the method works in general and check vertical and horizontal lines.

## Why Do These Three Steps Always Work? {/* #为什么这三步算出来的一定对 */}

Repeat the earlier calculation of $k,d$, now using coordinates for the two points. First consider the case where their horizontal coordinates differ.

Both points lie on $Y=kX+d$, so substituting each gives

$$
Y_1=kX_1+d,\qquad Y_2=kX_2+d.
$$

Subtract the first equation from the second to eliminate $d$:

$$
Y_2-Y_1=k(X_2-X_1).
$$

Since $X_2-X_1$ is nonzero, divide both sides by it:

$$
k=\frac{Y_2-Y_1}{X_2-X_1}.
$$

Now take any point $(X,Y)$ on the line. Subtract the first endpoint's equation $Y_1=kX_1+d$ from its equation $Y=kX+d$, again eliminating $d$:

$$
Y-Y_1=k(X-X_1).
$$

Substitute the value of $k$ just found:

$$
Y-Y_1=\frac{Y_2-Y_1}{X_2-X_1}(X-X_1).
$$

Multiply both sides by $X_2-X_1$ to clear the denominator:

$$
(X_2-X_1)(Y-Y_1)=(Y_2-Y_1)(X-X_1).
$$

Expand both sides with the distributive law:

$$
(X_2-X_1)Y-(X_2-X_1)Y_1
=(Y_2-Y_1)X-(Y_2-Y_1)X_1.
$$

Move the terms containing $X,Y$ to the left and the remaining terms to the right:

$$
(Y_2-Y_1)X-(X_2-X_1)Y
=(Y_2-Y_1)X_1-(X_2-X_1)Y_1.
$$

Note that $-(X_2-X_1)=X_1-X_2$. Thus the coefficient of $X$ on the left is exactly $a=Y_2-Y_1$, and the coefficient of $Y$ is exactly $b=X_1-X_2$. The right side is $aX_1+bY_1$, which is $c$.

This gives $aX+bY=c$. The subtraction order in the formula comes from rearranging the equation; it is not arbitrary.

## Does This Also Work for Vertical and Horizontal Lines? {/* #竖直线和水平线也能这样写吗 */}

**Yes for vertical lines, but they need a separate explanation.** We divided by $X_2-X_1$ above. Two points on a vertical line have equal horizontal coordinates, and division by $0$ is not allowed.

The figure below places the two special cases in one coordinate system. First consider the vertical blue line. From $(2,1)$ to $(2,4)$, the vertical coordinate changes, but the horizontal coordinate remains $2$, so its equation is $\teachBlue{X}=\teachBlue{2}$.

<ThemedImage
  alt="The vertical line X=2 passes through (2,1) and (2,4), keeping the horizontal coordinate fixed. The horizontal line Y=3 passes through (1,3) and (4,3), keeping the vertical coordinate fixed."
  sources={{
    light: useBaseUrl('/img/segment-intersection/line-equation/light/line-equation-axis-lines-light.svg'),
    dark: useBaseUrl('/img/segment-intersection/line-equation/dark/line-equation-axis-lines-dark.svg'),
  }}
/>

This also explains why $Y=kX+d$ cannot describe a vertical line: when $X=2$, the right side produces only one value, $2k+d$, which cannot equal both $1$ and $4$ from the figure.

Apply the three-step formula:

$$
a=4-1=3,\qquad b=2-2=0,\qquad c=3\times2+0\times1=6.
$$

We obtain $3X+0Y=6$, or $X=2$, which is correct. For any two distinct endpoints with the same horizontal coordinate, $b=0$ and $a\ne0$. The equation becomes $aX=aX_1$; dividing by $a$ gives $X=X_1$.

**Horizontal lines work too.** Consider the horizontal orange line: from $(1,3)$ to $(4,3)$, the horizontal coordinate changes but the vertical coordinate stays $3$, so the equation should be $\teachOrange{Y}=\teachOrange{3}$.

Substitution into the formula gives $a=0$, $b=-3$, and $c=-9$. The equation is therefore $-3Y=-9$, or $Y=3$.

Thus $aX+bY=c$ expresses ordinary slanted lines, horizontal lines, and vertical lines in the same form.

## Finally, Distinguish Two Issues {/* #最后分清两个问题 */}

**The endpoints must be distinct points.** If they coincide, the formula gives $a=b=c=0$ and the equation becomes $0=0$. Every point satisfies it, so it cannot specify a particular line. The geometry agrees: through a single known point, we can draw many different lines.

**A line equation describes the entire extended line.** In the figure below, the thick solid part is only the segment from $A=(1,3)$ to $B=(3,7)$. Dashed portions at both ends show that the line continues.

<ThemedImage
  alt="Segment AB is thick and solid; the line beyond its endpoints is dashed. P=(4,9) lies on the extension beyond B. Projections of A and B onto the horizontal axis mark the segment's horizontal range from 1 to 3, including both ends."
  sources={{
    light: useBaseUrl('/img/segment-intersection/line-equation/light/line-equation-segment-light.svg'),
    dark: useBaseUrl('/img/segment-intersection/line-equation/dark/line-equation-segment-dark.svg'),
  }}
/>

Consider $P=(4,9)$ in the figure. Substitution gives $9=2\times4+1$, a true equality, so it lies on the line. But it is beyond $B$, outside the thick solid segment.

Follow the two vertical guides downward: the horizontal coordinates of $A,B$ are $1,3$. Therefore this example requires both

$$
Y=2X+1,\qquad 1\le X\le3,
$$

to describe the original segment. The two equality signs in the bounds include its endpoints. **The equation determines the line containing the points; the coordinate bounds then select which part of it to take.** We use horizontal bounds here because this line is not vertical. For a vertical line, use the endpoints' vertical coordinates to bound the segment instead.
